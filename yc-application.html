Q1 · Moat & unfair advantage

What is your moat / unfair advantage?

Answer

Everyone in this space sells a snapshot — a lab sells a blood test, a DNA company sells a one-time report, a wearable sells a dashboard, a fitness app sells workouts. None of them connect those layers, and none of them prove the plan actually worked. We own the full loop: measure → interpret → intervene → retest → learn.

That loop is what compounds. Every user gives us a baseline, the protocol we prescribed, what they adhered to, how their wearables responded, and the retest delta — stratified by their genetics and phenotype. No single-modality company (a lab, a DNA startup, a wearable) can assemble that dataset, because none of them own the whole loop. With every cycle our reasoning engine gets sharper and the dataset gets harder to catch — that's the moat we're building, and we don't pretend to have it yet.

Two things let us build it faster than incumbents: we've already shipped a working, auditable reasoning engine as a solo founder (a real product, not a mockup), and we're starting in India, where we can run the full measure-and-retest loop far cheaper and faster than anyone in the US — so we generate outcome data per dollar that nobody else can match. The market is global; India is where we earn the proof.

Why it lands
  1. Leads with the insight, not the product — "everyone sells a snapshot; the value is the loop" is the thing you understand that competitors act like they don't.
  2. The moat is the dataset the loop produces — framed honestly as "building, not yet held." A partner trusts how a moat forms far more than a claim to already have one.
  3. Genetics is demoted to an input ("stratified by their genetics") — it enriches the data, it isn't the moat. This sidesteps the Nucleus comparison you'd lose.
  4. Your two real edges stated plainly — you shipped, and India = more outcome data per dollar. No hype, no "age reversal."
Q2 · Competition

Who are your competitors, and why are you better?

Answer

We take competition seriously — this is a hot space with well-funded players. In the US: Function Health (~$365/yr, 200k+ members) and Superpower ($199/yr, YC) on the blood-testing side, Nucleus and 23andMe on DNA, Oura/Whoop on wearables, InsideTracker/Levels on interpretation, and Fountain Life / Next Health on the clinic end. In India, there's effectively no direct competitor — the incumbent is the ₹5,000–15,000 "full-body checkup" from Tata 1mg / Healthians / Redcliffe, which hands you a PDF nobody knows how to act on.

We're not trying to out-brand Function in the US — they're years and millions ahead there, and we'd lose that fight today. We win on a different axis. Every one of those players owns one layer and sells a snapshot: a test, a DNA report, a ring, a dashboard. Even the best of them stops at "here are your numbers." We're the layer above all of them — we fuse blood + DNA + wearables + lifestyle into one record, reason over it with an auditable engine, prescribe a specific protocol, and retest to prove it moved. They optimize the sale of a measurement; we optimize the proven change and the outcome data it produces.

And we're doing it in India, where none of them operate and where we can run that full measure-and-retest loop at a fraction of US cost. So our beachhead is uncontested, our product is a category above the incumbents, and our unit economics for generating outcome data are structurally better. The US is the eventual prize; India is where we win first and cheapest.

Why it lands
  1. Names strong competitors on purpose — "we have no competitors" fails the question instantly; naming them signals you know the market cold.
  2. Concedes you don't beat Function in the US today — that honesty buys credibility and defuses the "we funded Superpower, why you?" objection before it's asked.
  3. Moves the fight to an axis you can win — you're a category above (the loop), not a better ring. That's how a small player survives giants.
  4. Reframes India as open field, not a small market — "the incumbent is a PDF nobody acts on" is a painful, obvious gap you fill.
Q3 · Why now

Why now?

Answer

Three inputs to this got cheap and abundant in the last few years: a 70-marker blood panel that used to need a doctor's referral and cost a fortune is now a $150–300 walk-in; consumer DNA is commoditized and millions already have a raw file; and wearables crossed into mass penetration, so continuous physiology is a free input, not a research device.

But cheap data was never the bottleneck — interpretation was. Turning blood + DNA + wearables + lifestyle into one safe, personalized, continuously-updated protocol used to require a doctor's brain per user, which doesn't scale. That's why the whole industry stops at "here are your numbers." LLMs are the unlock: for the first time you can build that reasoning layer — and, critically, make it auditable — instead of hiring a physician per member. The data got cheap years ago; the reasoning became buildable just now. That's the real "why now," and it's the part most people miss.

On the demand side, biological-age and biomarker awareness went mainstream (Function's 200k members, Whoop culture), so we're not creating global demand from scratch — and India's diagnostic rails (cheap labs, home draws) matured at the same moment, opening our beachhead.

Why it lands
  1. Refuses the generic "wearables + AI + cheap labs" — concedes it in one line, then pivots to the sharp version.
  2. The bottleneck was interpretation, and AI just removed it — a non-obvious insight, which is exactly what this question rewards.
  3. Explains why incumbents "stop at the numbers" — quietly justifying your whole product as the thing the moment enables.
  4. Timing on both product and market — the tech became buildable and India's rails matured at once.
Q4 · Unique insight

What do you understand that others don't?

Answer

The whole industry is racing to sell a cheaper snapshot — Function at $365, Superpower at $199, labs at cost. We think that's the wrong game. The test is a commodity, and nobody re-buys a report. The durable business isn't running the cheapest panel; it's owning the interpretation and the retest loop — the part that turns a number into an action and then proves the action worked. Whoever owns that layer wins; whoever races to the bottom on panel price is selling a vitamin nobody refills.

A second thing we get that most DNA-health companies don't: the genome is a prior, not a verdict. They lead with genetics and scare people with lifetime risk. We treat DNA as something that sharpens the read on your actual blood and behavior — it never overrides measured reality. That's safer, more honest, and it's why our engine tightens a target or changes a supplement form on a gene, but never diagnoses on one.

Put together: the industry sells measurements; we sell proven change and the compounding outcome data that comes from it.

Why it lands
  1. It's genuinely contrarian — the whole visible market competes on panel price; you say that's the losing game and can defend it (nobody refills a one-time report).
  2. Converts your weakness into a thesis — you can't win the price war, so you reframe the price war as the wrong game. Judo, not spin.
  3. "Genome is a prior, not a verdict" proves depth — a small, true, technical insight that separates you from Nucleus/23andMe's fear-first framing.
  4. Lands on the same closing line — measurements vs. proven change. The partner has now heard your one idea in four different words.

Q6 · Getting users

How will you get users?

Answer

First 10–15 (now): founder-led and warm, through the one channel that already aggregates our exact user — a doctor. GPs, cardiologists, and endocrinologists see anxious 40-somethings with borderline reports every day, can only hand them a prescription, and have nothing for the patient who wants to fix it through lifestyle. We take those patients, build a doctor-reviewed protocol from their labs, and retest in 90 days. One partner doctor is a steady feed of pre-qualified, already-worried, willing-to-pay users. In parallel we mine our own network with a single question — "who do you know, 40+, doing well, who just got a scary health report?" — which surfaces warm, high-intent intros.

We deliberately don't lean on content or cold outreach for the first cohort. In a new category with no brand, those don't convert — we've tested it. We convert on trust: a 30-minute consult where we show the person exactly what our engine sees in their own data.

Scaling: every happy member is asked for two referrals and becomes a case study; we add more doctors and premium gyms (each a node sitting on dozens of our users); and once we have outcome proof, we expand to the Indian diaspora and the US, where willingness-to-pay is already proven. Long term, the same engine becomes the layer clinics and gyms run their members through — distribution that compounds.

Why it lands
  1. Specific and un-generic — "one partner doctor who already sits on our exact user" beats "content and paid ads," which 90% of applicants write.
  2. Shows you know what doesn't work — "we tested content/cold outreach; they don't convert in a new category" is a founder who has actually tried things.
  3. Answers "will they pay?" inside the channel — routing through a scared patient with a bad report means you're selling a painkiller, not a vitamin.
  4. Believable path from 10 → many → moat — referrals → more nodes → diaspora/US → infrastructure, ending on the same distribution-moat thesis.
