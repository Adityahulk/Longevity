The Zone 2
glucose paradox.

Endurance, exercise-induced hyperglycemia and the skeletal-muscle glucose sink.

21 September 2026· Antiaging Labs Field Notes· 11 min read

Low-intensity steady-state work, often called Zone 2, is foundational for mitochondrial efficiency and oxidative capacity. But high-volume cardio alone does not guarantee optimal glucose control.

Continuous glucose monitor data can reveal a counterintuitive pattern in long-distance runners and cyclists: a temporary rise in glucose during, or immediately after, a prolonged workout. Even with a high calorie burn, glucose clearance can be delayed.

The key idea

Endurance can build a powerful aerobic engine while the body is temporarily putting more glucose into circulation than working muscle is taking out.

Understanding this begins with the stress response to prolonged exercise, then follows glucose all the way into the muscle cell.

1. The physiology of exercise-induced hyperglycemia

During low-to-moderate intensity exercise, at a true Zone 2 effort, energy demand is primarily met through lipid oxidation. Muscle glucose uptake and liver glucose production are broadly matched.

As duration increases, or intensity drifts upward during a long run, the neuroendocrine response shifts.

The prolonged-workout pathway
  1. Adrenal activation and catecholamine surge. Extended physical stress stimulates the sympathetic nervous system and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, raising epinephrine, norepinephrine and cortisol.
  2. Uncoupled liver glucose output. Epinephrine binds to beta-2 adrenergic receptors on hepatocytes and activates glycogen phosphorylase. The liver speeds up glycogenolysis, breaking stored glycogen into glucose, and gluconeogenesis, creating glucose from non-carbohydrate substrates such as lactate and amino acids.
  3. The production-disposal mismatch. The liver can release glucose faster than working slow-twitch Type I fibers take it up. The result is elevated blood glucose during and immediately after the run.

2. Hormonal interference and temporary insulin resistance

High systemic cortisol does more than increase liver glucose production. It also creates a physiological environment that temporarily blunts insulin sensitivity.

Cortisol-induced blunting

Insulin signalling is dampened

Elevated cortisol can impair the usual insulin signalling cascade in non-exercising tissues by inhibiting Akt phosphorylation, making standard insulin-mediated glucose uptake less effective.

FFA competition

The Randle Cycle

Prolonged endurance exercise raises circulating free fatty acids. These fatty acids compete with glucose for cellular oxidation, temporarily reducing glucose clearance in resting muscle after the workout.

3. How glucose enters muscle: insulin versus contraction

Heavy strength work matters in this context because muscle cells can bring glucose in through two routes. Both lead to GLUT4, the glucose transporter that moves from inside the cell to its surface.

Two routes, one transporter

Insulin and contraction use different signalling routes. Both result in GLUT4 reaching the cell membrane, where glucose can enter the muscle cell.

Path A · insulin-dependent

The resting route

Insulin binds to its receptor on the muscle-cell membrane, triggering the sequence IR to IRS-1 to PI3K to Akt. GLUT4 vesicles fuse with the membrane, allowing glucose to enter down its concentration gradient.

Path B · contraction-mediated

The mechanical route

High-force contractions bypass the insulin pathway. Mechanical strain, ATP turnover and calcium flux activate AMPK and CaMK, directly moving GLUT4 to the membrane.

The mechanical advantageHeavy lifting and explosive resistance recruit high-threshold Type II motor units, creating a robust, insulin-independent route for glucose clearance that remains elevated for hours after exercise.

4. Skeletal muscle is the body’s largest glucose sink

Skeletal muscle is responsible for 70 to 80 percent of total post-meal glucose disposal. Relying only on endurance work without building or preserving muscle volume can create a structural bottleneck in metabolic capacity.

Muscle stores glucose as glycogen. More total muscle cross-sectional area creates a larger reservoir to absorb glucose surges from liver output and dietary carbohydrates.

Type II fibers have high concentrations of glycolytic enzymes and GLUT4 transporters. Neglecting these fibers through exclusive long-distance cardio reduces the density of available glucose-clearance pathways.

5. A practical protocol for the Zone 2 glucose paradox

The aim is to maintain cardiovascular endurance while improving total glucose disposal and reducing post-workout hyperglycemia.

Heavy compound lifting, two to three times a week

Use multi-joint movements such as squats, deadlifts, presses and rows at 75 to 85 percent of one-repetition maximum. The goal is to trigger mechanical GLUT4 translocation while maintaining muscle cross-sectional area.

Add brief tension after a long Zone 2 session

After a long session, use two or three short high-intensity intervals or a brief set of resistance movements, such as heavy kettlebell swings or air squats, to clear circulating glucose before recovery.

Match carbohydrate to the workload

During low-intensity Zone 2 work, over-consuming high-glycemic intra-workout sugars can compound liver glucose release and create unnecessary glucose elevation. Align carbohydrate intake with the actual workload.

Cardio builds oxidative capacity. Muscle mass and mechanical tension expand the glucose sink.
